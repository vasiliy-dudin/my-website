# Quality Criteria for CV Content, Case Studies, and Website Copy

## The core test for any piece of content

**The specificity test:** every *claim* should have at least two of these three:

* A number or metric ("adopted by 8 product teams", "12 usability sessions")
* A named tool, method, or framework ("design tokens in Figma", "RITE method")
* A named context ("a B2B platform with 200K+ business users", "a zero-to-one billing flow redesign")

**Scope:** this applies to claims about my work and contribution. It does not apply to connective prose, transitions, or a sentence that sets up a scene. A case study written entirely in specificity-test sentences reads like a spreadsheet. The test is a filter for claims, not a template for every line.

Illustrative examples (placeholders — replace with real ones from my own materials once the CV and case study texts are in the project):

* Fails: "Improved the user experience."
* Passes: "Reduced task completion time from 7 minutes to 90 seconds for a B2B onboarding flow, measured through unmoderated usability testing."

## Attribution: how to claim work honestly

Over-claiming and under-claiming are both disqualifying. The rule is to name the scope of ownership, not to reach for the strongest available verb.

| **Situation**                                 | **Use**                                           | **Do not use**                           |
| :-------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ | :--------------------------------------- |
| I owned the design work end to end            | Designed, Owned, Led design on                    | —                                        |
| I led a team effort as one of several leads   | Led \[my part], Drove \[specific decision]        | Led (unqualified, for the whole project) |
| I was one contributor among several designers | Designed \[specific surface], Partnered with X on | Led, Drove, Spearheaded                  |
| I influenced a decision I didn't own          | Proposed, Advocated for, Pushed back on           | Led, Owned, Decided                      |



"Led" is legitimate when the object of the verb is specific and true: "Led design for a 7-person Scrum team" is a claim I can defend in an interview. "Led the redesign" when four other people co-owned it is the over-claiming red flag. When in doubt, narrow the object rather than weaken the verb.

Weak verbs to avoid entirely (they describe presence, not contribution): worked on, helped, supported, assisted, involved in, responsible for.

Strong verbs by domain:

* Design systems: Architected, Systematised, Standardised, Governed, Scaled, Documented, Migrated
* Accessibility: Audited, Remediated, Established (standards/guidelines)
* Research and validation: Prototyped, Validated, Synthesised, Facilitated, Recruited
* Product design: Designed, Defined, Scoped, Mapped, Tested, Shipped, Iterated
* Collaboration/leadership: Led, Aligned, Presented, Mentored, Coordinated

## Metric provenance

Every number in a CV or case study must be traceable to one of three states. Track this while drafting, not after:

* **Verified** — I can point to the source (analytics, a report, a document)
* **From my notes** — my own record, not independently verifiable, but real
* **Unverified** — a placeholder, an estimate, or a figure of unknown origin

Unverified numbers never ship. If a figure cannot be moved into one of the first two states, remove it and describe the change qualitatively instead. A case study with no numbers is survivable; a case study with a number I can't defend in an interview is not.

## Criteria for strong CV bullet points

A strong bullet:

* Opens with a specific action verb (see attribution table above)
* Describes scope and context, not just activity
* Reflects my actual contribution, distinguishable from the team's
* Includes outcome or impact — quantified where possible, qualitative where metrics don't exist
* Uses vocabulary matching how the role is described in target job descriptions
* Makes sense to a hiring manager who doesn't know the company or product

---

## Criteria for a strong professional summary

A strong summary:

* Names the specific type of designer, not "experienced product designer"
* Specifies the products and problems I work on (B2B SaaS, design systems)
* States the value I bring concretely (design/engineering bridge, accessibility depth, systems thinking)
* Is under 60 words
* Would not fit another designer without changing at least half the words

## Criteria for strong portfolio case study content

Applies to featured case studies (tier 1 below). A strong case study:

* Makes the problem clear before any solution — why it mattered to users *and* to the business
* Shows my own thinking and decisions, not just the team's output
* Explains what options were considered and why one was chosen
* Shows evidence of contact with users, even if lightweight
* Describes constraints honestly: time, technical, organisational, resource
* Shows what actually changed — in user behaviour, product metrics, or business outcomes. "Stakeholders were happy" is not an outcome.
* Is scannable in layers: the story from headings and first sentences alone in under 60 seconds; full detail available to whoever wants it
* Survives being walked through out loud in about 10 minutes. If I can't narrate it without reading the page, the structure is wrong — the same case has to work as an interview presentation.

A weak case study is: brief → wireframes → final screens, with no decisions, no research, no outcomes.

### When there are no positive metrics

A project that didn't move its metrics is publishable and can be a senior signal, but only with all four of these present:

1. The metrics shown honestly, not buried or reframed
2. An explanation of *why* the hypothesis didn't hold, based on real post-launch investigation — not speculation
3. What the team did as a result — the decision the work informed
4. What I would do differently, specifically

Missing any one of these turns an honest case into an unexplained failure. Placement matters too: this works as a second or third featured case, not as the first thing a reviewer opens.

## Criteria for the portfolio as a whole

The portfolio has two tiers, and they are held to different standards. The mistake to avoid is applying case study depth to everything (unsustainable) or letting lighter work sit at the same visual weight as the featured work (dilutes it).

### Tier 1 — featured case studies

2–3 projects, fully written to the case study criteria above.

* **Lead case:** the first one a reviewer opens should have the clearest outcome and the strongest evidence of my own decisions. A no-positive-metrics project belongs second or third, where the cases around it frame it as honesty rather than as the best I have.
* **Coverage:** across tier 1, each area of depth in user\_profile — design systems, accessibility, user research — should appear in at least one case. A specialisation claimed in the profile but absent from every featured case is a credibility gap. Tier 2 can support a specialisation but not carry it alone.
* **Range:** not three variations of the same problem type.

### Tier 2 — additional work

A shorter section below the featured work: AI projects, side projects, visual/UI work shown mainly as screens, and writing. This tier exists to widen the picture, not to pad it.

Rules:

* **Framing is the protection.** Screens without a case study read as a junior signal only when they're presented as if they were a case study. Under a heading that plainly says what this section is, the same screens read as range. Label the section so no reviewer has to guess.
* **Each item needs one line of context:** what it was, what my role was, and why it's here. An item with no line of context is decoration.
* **Each item earns its place by showing something tier 1 doesn't** — a different domain, a tool, self-direction, writing ability, or a specialisation the featured cases underweight. If it only repeats what tier 1 already proves, cut it.
* **Cap the tail.** Roughly 3–6 items. A long list of weak entries lowers the average impression of the work; reviewers judge the set, not the best item in it.
* **Visual hierarchy must be unambiguous.** Tier 2 entries are smaller, denser, and clearly secondary. If a reviewer can't tell within a second which projects I'm asking to be judged on, the hierarchy has failed.
* **No dead ends.** Every tier 2 item either links somewhere real (a live product, an article, a repo, a prototype) or is self-contained on the page. A tile that goes nowhere and explains nothing is worse than absence.

For AI projects specifically: the signal a reviewer looks for is judgment about the technology — what it was appropriate for, where it failed, what the interaction model had to account for. "I used AI" is not itself the point. If an AI project has real users and a real outcome, it may belong in tier 1 rather than here.

For side projects: include only where they show something the paid work can't — self-directed scope, a domain I don't otherwise cover, or shipped end-to-end ownership. A side project that repeats the day job adds nothing.

For writing: an article earns a place if it demonstrates thinking a reviewer would want on their team. A thin post is a negative signal, not a neutral one. Link out rather than reproducing full text on the site.

### Entry point

The homepage answers "who is this, what have they worked on, is this the right level" without a click. Tier 1 is what's visible first; tier 2 is reachable but never competing for that first look.

## Criteria for visuals and image content

Visual craft is assumed at senior level; the differentiator is whether each image is doing a job.

* Every image in a featured case illustrates a specific decision or finding. An image that only shows that work happened should be cut. (Tier 2 is the place for work that is legitimately just screens.)
* Every image in a featured case has a caption saying what to look at and why it matters — not a label describing what it is. "Final dashboard" is a label. "The share trigger moved inline after 8 of 12 participants missed it in the toolbar" is a caption.
* Screens containing UI text are in English. Untranslated interface copy makes the work unreadable to the audience it's aimed at.
* **Alt text** on every meaningful image: describes the content and its purpose in context, not "screenshot" or "design". Decorative images are marked as decorative rather than given filler descriptions.
* The site itself meets WCAG 2.1 AA. Given that accessibility is a claimed specialisation, the portfolio is itself evidence — a site failing contrast or keyboard navigation contradicts the claim more loudly than any case study supports it.

## Criteria for strong website copy

**Hero/intro:**

* Says who I am and what problems I solve in one or two sentences
* Specific to me, not a generic senior designer description
* Works for a hiring manager who knows nothing about me

**About page:**

* Describes my actual approach in specific terms, not "I love solving complex problems"
* Names the types of teams and products I work best in
* First person, sounds like a person talking
* Under 200 words — detail lives in case studies

**Project card descriptions (tier 1):**

* Problem → role → outcome in 2–3 sentences
* Specific enough that someone knows what the project was about without clicking
* No marketing language

**Project card descriptions (tier 2):**

* One line. What it was, my role, why it's worth a look.
* Written so it's obvious this is a shorter form by intent, not an unfinished case study.

## Signals that distinguish senior from mid-level work

Senior signals:

* Ambiguity handled: unclear brief, no precedent, competing stakeholder needs
* Cross-functional influence: changed how a team worked, not just what it built
* Systems thinking: decisions accounting for edge cases, future states, scale
* Mentoring other designers, even informally
* Proposing solutions rather than executing requests
* Trade-offs named — what was left on the table, and why that was acceptable
* Understanding of engineering and business constraints, not just user needs

Mid-level signals (avoid over-emphasising):

* Execution quality on defined tasks
* Following an established process
* Individual screen-level decisions
* Tool proficiency

## Red flags

* All bullets describe activity with no outcome
* Summary describes personality traits instead of professional positioning
* Polished UI presented as a case study, with no decisions or context
* Language that could describe any designer at any company
* Over-claiming contribution (see the attribution table above)
* Under-claiming: not owning work that was genuinely mine
* Buzzwords and clichés — see the banned/flagged word lists (the portfolio-case-study-writing skill's quality-flags.md). That list is the single source of truth; do not maintain a second copy here.
* Every case study ending happily, with no constraints, failures, or learnings
* Numbers whose origin I can't state (see metric provenance above)
* Tier 2 work sitting at the same visual weight as featured work
* A specialisation claimed in user\_profile document with no featured case behind it
* Process-heavy case studies that never arrive at an outcome
* No mention of engineering handoff or implementation reality anywhere in a  featured case

## Spelling and language conventions

All written materials use British English: *organised, prioritised, specialisation, behaviour, centred.*

One exception: ATS keyword matching. Job descriptions from US-headquartered companies often use US spellings, and some terms are effectively fixed strings — "User-Centered Design" is the common ATS form. Where a term is being used as a keyword and the spellings diverge, match the job description rather than the style rule, and note it as a deliberate exception rather than an inconsistency.
